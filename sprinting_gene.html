Is there a ‘Sprinting Gene’?

ACTN3 R577X — Lock5 Phase 1 Workflow Test

Author

Lock5Stat-by-AI

Published

October 21, 2026

Question

Is there a “sprinting gene”? The α-actinin-3 gene (ACTN3) encodes a protein expressed in fast-twitch muscle fibers. The R577X polymorphism creates a premature stop codon: the XX genotype produces no functioning actinin-3 at all.

Data: @yang2003 — genotype distributions for elite sprint athletes (n=107), endurance athletes (n=194), and controls (n=436). This is the same case study used in Statistics: Unlocking the Power of Data (Lock⁵).

Data

ACTN3 R577X genotype counts (Yang et al. 2003)
group n RR RX XX
Control 436 130 226 80
Sprint 107 53 48 6
Endurance 194 60 88 46

Exploratory analysis

Elite sprinters show a markedly lower proportion of the XX genotype (5.6% vs 18.3% in controls) and a higher R-allele frequency (72.0% vs 55.7%).

Hypothesis tests

H₀: ACTN3 genotype distribution is the same for sprinters and controls Hₐ: distributions differ

Chi-square test of homogeneity: χ² = 19.54, df = 2, p = 0.000057

Randomization test (Lock5 approach)

We simulate 100,000 shuffles of group labels under H₀ and recompute χ² each time:

Permutation test p-value = 0.00004

Two-proportion test: XX genotype

Sprinters XX: 6/107 = 0.056 vs Controls: 80/436 = 0.183
z = -3.235, p-value = 0.00122

Interval estimate: R-allele advantage

Bootstrap 95% CI for the difference in R-allele frequency (sprint − control):

95% CI: (0.093, 0.231)

Decision

Method Result p-value / CI
χ² homogeneity (Sprint vs Control) χ² = 19.54, df = 2 p < 0.0001
Randomization test (100k shuffles) — p < 0.0001
Two-proportion z (XX genotype) z = −3.24 p = 0.0012
Bootstrap 95% CI (R-allele diff) — (0.093, 0.231)

Conclusion: All three methods agree — reject H₀. Genotype distribution differs significantly between elite sprinters and controls. The XX (no-actinin-3) genotype is under-represented among elite sprinters (5.6% vs 18.3%), and sprinters carry the R allele at a rate ~16 percentage points higher than controls.

Statistical answer: yes — there is strong evidence for a “sprinting gene” association. Caveats: observational data (association ≠ causation), modest sprinter sample size (only 6 XX sprinters), and elite athletic performance involves many genes and environmental factors.
